Replication study

What counts as a heatwave in Phoenix?

A replication of the UK Met Office's percentile-based heatwave definition (McCarthy et al., 2019), applied to 75 years of daily temperature data from Phoenix Sky Harbor.

Source paper. McCarthy, M., Armstrong, L., & Armstrong, N. (2019). A new heatwave definition for the UK. Weather, 74(11), 382–387.

Data. NOAA NCEI GHCN-Daily, station GHCND:USW00023183. Climatological baseline: 1981–2010.

01

The threshold curve

For every day of the year, the algorithm computes the 90th percentile of historical maximum temperatures using a 15-day rolling window across the 1981–2010 baseline. Anything above this line was, historically, an unusually hot day for that time of year.

90th percentile threshold

A 95°F day in January falls well above this curve and counts as unusual. A 95°F day in July falls well below it and is routine. The same temperature, different meaning depending on time of year. This locality-aware behavior is the entire reason percentile-based definitions exist.

03

The trend the methodology exposes

When the baseline is held fixed at 1981–2010 and the climate shifts beneath it, the count of detected heatwaves rises decade over decade. McCarthy et al. anticipated this — it is the reason the Met Office updated its UK baseline to 1991–2020 in 2022.

The algorithm is not becoming more sensitive over time. The threshold is computed once, from a fixed historical window, and never updated. What is changing is the actual temperature distribution — recent decades systematically exceed the late-20th-century 90th percentile. The decade trend is, in effect, a measurement of climate drift relative to a frozen reference frame.
